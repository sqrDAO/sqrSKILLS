## Cold email: BASHO / WYWYN

A BASHO email is a researched, one-to-one email. Its job is not to sell, book a demo, or explain the product. Its job is to earn a reply. Judge drafts against reply rate, never open rate.

**WYWYN** is the test every BASHO email must pass: **Why You, Why You Now.** If the same email could be sent to a different person, or sent six months from now, unchanged, it is not a BASHO email. It is a mail merge.

**Structure, five parts, under 120 words:**

1. **Trigger.** A specific, verifiable, recent event: their funding round, a hire, a launch, a regulatory change in their market, something they said publicly. This is the *Why You Now*.
2. **Relevance bridge.** One sentence connecting the trigger to a problem people in their seat usually have as a result. This is the *Why You*.
3. **Value hypothesis.** A claim, hedged honestly, about the outcome. Ideally anchored to a comparable company. "For two other mid-market operators in the same corridor, this cut settlement from three days to same-day."
4. **Interest-based CTA.** Ask for a reaction, not a calendar slot. "Is settlement time actually on your list this year, or am I off?" A yes/no question that costs three seconds to answer outperforms "do you have 15 minutes Tuesday" by a wide margin, because it does not ask for a commitment before value is established.
5. **Signature.** No attachments, no links on the first touch, no calendar embed.

**Failure modes:** opening with "I" or your company name; a paragraph about the product; false familiarity ("hope you're crushing it"); a trigger so generic it is insulting ("saw you're in fintech"); more than one ask.

**Example.**

> Subject: three-day settlement on the VN corridor
>
> Saw you added two corridor partners last month.
>
> Usually when operators expand corridors that fast, the correspondent-fee line grows faster than volume does, and it lands on ops rather than finance.
>
> Two operators your size cut settlement from three days to same-day on their VN leg, which took roughly 60bps out of the cost line.
>
> Is corridor cost actually on your list for this year, or am I reading it wrong?

Note that the CTA is answerable with one word, and that "or am I reading it wrong" gives a graceful exit. Making it easy to say no is what makes it possible to say yes.

## The 20-second cold call pitch

Twenty seconds is roughly 55 words. It buys you the right to ask a question. Nothing more.

**Structure:**
1. **Permission.** "This is a cold call, do you want to hang up, or give me twenty seconds?" It is disarming precisely because it is honest, and it earns disproportionate patience.
2. **Who and why.** One sentence: who you work with, and the problem you solve for them. In their language.
3. **The question.** A specific, pain-oriented, open question. Then stop talking.

**Example.**

> "Hi Minh, this is a cold call. Want to hang up, or give me twenty seconds?
> ...
> Thanks. We work with mid-market remittance operators in Southeast Asia who are watching correspondent fees eat their corridor margin. Nine times out of ten the ops lead has already flagged it and nobody has the budget to fix it.
> How are you handling the VN leg right now?"

**Rules:** never open with "how are you today"; never ask "is now a bad time" (it invites the answer you do not want); never pitch the product on the first call; the goal of the call is one qualified question answered and a next step, and if you get either one, the call worked.

## Touches: the cadence

A single touch is not outreach. Reply rates cluster around touches four through eight, which is exactly where most sellers have already quit.

A workable default over roughly three weeks, multi-channel, escalating personalization:

| # | Day | Channel | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Email | BASHO, trigger-based |
| 2 | 3 | LinkedIn | Connect, no pitch, no note or a one-liner referencing the trigger |
| 3 | 5 | Call | 20-second pitch, voicemail if no answer |
| 4 | 8 | Email | New angle, new trigger, not a "just bumping this" |
| 5 | 11 | Call | Different time of day |
| 6 | 14 | Email or channel they prefer | Value drop: a benchmark, a peer result, something useful even if they never reply |
| 7 | 18 | Referral | Reach a colleague; ask who owns this |
| 8 | 22 | Email | Polite close: "Assuming this is not a priority, I will stop here. Reopen anytime." |

Principles: never repeat an angle, always add something new. Vary channel and time of day. The closing touch is a real close, not a guilt trip, and it produces more replies than touches two through six combined. And every touch should be defensible if forwarded to the person's boss.

## Objection handling: the LAER loop

An objection is information delivered under mild social pressure. The reflex is to answer it. The reflex is wrong, because most stated objections are not the real objection, and answering the stated one closes the door on discovering the real one.

**L. Listen.** Fully. Do not begin composing a rebuttal.

**A. Acknowledge.** Legitimize the concern without conceding it. "That is a fair concern, and you are not the first person to raise it."

**E. Explore.** This is the whole method. Ask what sits underneath. "When you say it's too expensive, is it that the number is above what you can approve, or that the return is not clear enough to justify it?" Those two objections have opposite responses.

**R. Respond.** Only now. Address the objection you uncovered, not the one you were handed. Then check: "Does that address it, or is there something else?"

The one rule with teeth: never answer an objection you have not explored. It is faster and it feels productive and it loses deals.

## The objection library

Build one. Every objection you hear goes in it with the underlying cause, the exploring question, and the response that worked. Structure the tab as: *Objection heard* | *Likely underlying cause* | *Exploring question* | *Response* | *Proof asset*.

Most objections are one of six things wearing a costume.

**1. "Too expensive."** Usually means the value is not quantified, or you are talking to someone below the purse holder. Explore: is the number above your approval limit, or above what it is worth? If the first, you have an authority problem. If the second, you have a Stage 2 problem: go get their impact figure.

**2. "No budget."** Usually true and usually irrelevant, because budget is a route not a pool. Explore: is there no line item, or is the line item spent? Ask what they *did* fund this year and what made those things fundable. Reallocation from a cost line you shrink is the fastest route and is not really a budget conversation at all.

**3. "Send me some information."** Almost always a polite exit. Explore: what specifically would you want the information to answer? A real request produces a real question. A brush-off produces "just, you know, general information." Respect the brush-off; ask for permission to check back after a named trigger.

**4. "We already have a solution" / "We do it in-house."** Explore: what does the current approach do well? Genuinely. Then: what would have to break for you to revisit it? You are not looking to attack the incumbent, you are looking for the gap the incumbent's owner already privately knows about.

**5. "Not right now."** The urgency objection, and the most honest one. Explore: what would need to happen for this to become a this-quarter problem? Then either that event is on their roadmap, and you have a real date, or it is not, and this is not a deal this year. Say so in the forecast. A clean "not this year" is worth more than a dirty "maybe."

**6. "I need to talk to my team."** The authority objection. Explore: who specifically, and what will they ask you? Then offer to be in the room, and if refused, arm the champion with the exact material that answers what those people will ask. A champion sent into a room alone with a PDF loses.

**Crypto and Web3 addenda.** Common in that market and worth a permanent entry each: token/regulatory exposure ("our compliance team will not touch this"), counterparty and custody risk, mercenary-capital doubt ("your TVL leaves the day incentives stop"), and vendor longevity ("will you exist in eighteen months"). The first is explored as: is it a categorical policy, or a documentation gap? Those are very different objections, and the second one is solvable this quarter.
