# The Business Model Canvas

The canvas (Osterwalder and Pigneur, *Business Model Generation*, licensed by Strategyzer under CC BY-SA 3.0) is the one-page map of how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. It sits *above* the GTM stages in this playbook. Opportunity selection decides which idea to pursue; the canvas describes the whole business built around that idea; the direct-GTM stages then operationalize the customer-facing half of it.

The reason it belongs in this skill and not in a folder of its own: the canvas and the GTM playbook are the same territory at two zoom levels. Five of the nine blocks *are* the go-to-market. Filling the canvas first stops teams from building a beautiful persona map for a value proposition that no revenue stream pays for, or a partner matrix disconnected from any key activity the partner actually supplies.

## The two halves

Draw a line down the middle of the canvas, under Value Propositions. Everything to the **right** (Customer Segments, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams) is *value delivery and capture*: the market-facing surface. Everything to the **left** (Key Activities, Key Resources, Key Partners, Cost Structure) is *value creation*: the machine behind the curtain. Value Propositions is the hinge that joins them.

This is the single most useful thing the canvas does for a GTM plan. The right half is the go-to-market you are already building stage by stage. The left half is the part founders skip, and skipping it is why a plan can be internally perfect and still lose money: a value proposition the market wants, delivered through the right channel to the right segment, on the back of a cost structure that never breaks even.

So: fill the right half in lockstep with the GTM stages, and use the left half as the reality check.

## The nine blocks

Each block below carries the canvas's own guiding questions and its menu of standard options. Treat the options as prompts, not a checklist to exhaust.

### Value Propositions (the hinge)
*What value do we deliver? Which of the customer's problems do we solve? Which customer needs do we satisfy? What bundles do we offer each segment?*

Value comes in recognized flavors: newness, performance, customization, "getting the job done," design, brand/status, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, accessibility, convenience/usability. Name which one or two you lead with. A proposition that claims five is a proposition with no edge.

### Customer Segments (right)
*For whom are we creating value? Who are our most important customers?*

Archetypes: mass market, niche market, segmented, diversified, multi-sided platform. If you named a multi-sided platform here, you owe *two* value propositions and *two* revenue logics, one per side, and the canvas has to balance for both.

### Channels (right)
*Through which channels do segments want to be reached? Which work best and most cost-efficiently? How are they integrated into customer routines?*

The canvas breaks a channel into five phases, and a plan that only covers the first two is a marketing plan, not a channel: **Awareness** (how they hear of you), **Evaluation** (how they judge the value proposition), **Purchase** (how they buy), **Delivery** (how the value proposition reaches them), **After sales** (how you support them post-purchase).

### Customer Relationships (right)
*What relationship does each segment expect? How costly is it? How is it integrated with the rest of the model?*

Modes: personal assistance, dedicated personal assistance, self-service, automated services, communities, co-creation. The cost of the mode is a line item, not an afterthought: dedicated assistance and self-service produce completely different cost structures for the same revenue.

### Revenue Streams (right)
*What are customers really willing to pay for? What do they pay now? How are they paying, and how would they prefer to? How much does each stream contribute?*

Stream types: asset sale, usage fee, subscription, lending/renting/leasing, licensing, brokerage fees, advertising. Pricing splits into **fixed** (list price, product-feature dependent, segment dependent, volume dependent) and **dynamic** (negotiation, yield management, real-time market). The willingness-to-pay question is the one founders answer with a wish; hold it to evidence.

### Key Activities (left)
*What activities do our value propositions, channels, relationships, and revenue streams require?*

Categories: production, problem solving, platform/network. The activity that produces your lead value proposition is the one to protect; everything else is a candidate for a partner.

### Key Resources (left)
*What resources do our value propositions, channels, relationships, and revenue streams require?*

Types: physical, intellectual (brand, patents, copyrights, data), human, financial. In Web3 and platform businesses the decisive resource is usually intellectual or network, not physical; say which, because it dictates what you must own versus rent.

### Key Partners (left)
*Who are our key partners and suppliers? Which resources do we acquire from them? Which activities do they perform?*

Motivations for any partnership reduce to three: optimization and economy of scale, reduction of risk and uncertainty, or acquisition of particular resources and activities. Every partner row should trace to one of the three, and this block is the same object as the partnership matrix in `partnership-goals.md`, one zoomed out, one zoomed in.

### Cost Structure (left)
*What are the most important costs? Which key resources and activities are most expensive?*

A model is either **cost-driven** (leanest structure, low price, automation, outsourcing) or **value-driven** (premium proposition, personal service). Costs are fixed, variable, or subject to economies of scale or scope. Pick the driver deliberately; a value-driven proposition on a cost-driven structure starves the thing customers are paying for.

## How each block feeds the rest of the playbook

The canvas is not a parallel exercise. It hands specific inputs to specific stages. Fill a block, and you have started the stage it points to.

| Canvas block | Feeds | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Value Propositions | Offer definition | Stage 2 / `icp-personas.md` (qualifying: your solution) |
| Customer Segments | ICP and persona tiering | Stage 3 / `icp-personas.md` |
| Channels | Attention plan **and** the sales cycle's Delivery/After-sales stages | Stages 4 and 6 / `sales-cycle.md` |
| Customer Relationships | Relationship mode and its cost, which shapes methodology and cadence | Stages 5 and 7 |
| Revenue Streams | Willingness-to-pay and pricing, the economic spine of qualification | Stage 3 (target-market economics) |
| Key Partners | Partnership goal matrix | Stage 8 / `partnership-goals.md` |
| Key Activities / Resources / Cost | The reality check GTM has no other home for | This file |

Run the mapping in both directions during an audit. A persona in the plan with no matching customer segment on the canvas is a segment you are selling to but have not modeled. A revenue stream on the canvas that no persona is qualified against is money you have drawn but not sourced.

## Sequence: how to fill it

Do **not** fill left to right. Fill in the order the logic actually runs:

1. **Customer Segments** first. Everything anchors to who.
2. **Value Propositions** for each segment. The hinge.
3. **Channels**, then **Customer Relationships**: how the value proposition reaches and holds each segment.
4. **Revenue Streams**: what that delivery captures. The right half is now whole; sanity-check it against the GTM stages before crossing the line.
5. **Key Activities**, then **Key Resources**: what producing the right half demands.
6. **Key Partners**: which of those activities and resources you do not do yourself.
7. **Cost Structure** last, because only now do you know what everything costs.

Then read the whole canvas as one sentence and see whether it closes: *we deliver [value proposition] to [segment] through [channels], keeping them via [relationship], earning [revenue] from it, by doing [activities] with [resources], partnering for [the rest], at [cost].* If that sentence has a gap, that gap is the next customer conversation.

As with the rest of the playbook, do not invent answers to fill blocks. `TBD` on a block is a research target. A plausible fabrication is a landmine you bury for month six.

## Where canvases go wrong

Add these to the audit pass. Ordered by how often they sink a plan.

1. **Right half without a left half.** A fully worked market-facing story and three empty creation blocks. The plan cannot be costed, so it cannot be judged. Blocker.
2. **Value proposition claiming everything.** Five value flavors, no lead. If it is newness *and* price *and* design *and* status, it is a brochure. Gap.
3. **Segment / persona mismatch.** A persona in the GTM plan with no segment on the canvas, or a segment with no persona. Gap.
4. **Revenue stream without willingness-to-pay evidence.** "Subscription" written with no answer to what they pay now and what they would prefer. This is the most common self-deception on the canvas. Blocker if it is the primary stream.
5. **Channel that stops at Evaluation.** Awareness and evaluation filled, delivery and after-sales blank. That is a demand-gen plan wearing a channel's clothes. Gap.
6. **Cost driver contradicts the value proposition.** Premium, personal-service value proposition sitting on a cost-driven, maximum-automation structure, or the reverse. Blocker: one of the two is a lie.
7. **Partner with no motivation.** A key partner who maps to none of the three motivations (economy, risk reduction, resource/activity acquisition) is a logo you want on a slide, not a partner. Nit unless it is load-bearing.
8. **Relationship mode with no cost consequence.** "Communities" or "dedicated assistance" named in Customer Relationships but absent from Cost Structure. Nit, but it is where margin quietly dies.

Lead the audit with the sentence-closes test above. If the model reads as one coherent sentence, the blocks are talking to each other, which is the entire point of putting them on one page.
